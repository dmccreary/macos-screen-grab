    Quiz: Total Cost of Ownership¶

    Test your understanding of purchase price, multi-year cost, opportunity cost, maintenance, learning cost, and total cost of ownership with these review questions.

    1. What is a break-even point?¶

    1. The price at which a vendor stops making a profit
    2. The moment at which the total costs of two options become equal, after which one of them is cheaper
    3. The date on which a subscription renews
    4. The purchase price divided by the number of computers
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. A break-even point is where two options cost the same in total. After it, one option is cheaper. For a generated tool compared with a subscription, it is the time when fees saved equal what you spent on the tool. Using only purchase prices, the $14 tool breaks even against $90 per year in fees after about 0.16 years. Options A, C, and D describe other ideas.

    See: Multi-Year Cost

    2. What is opportunity cost?¶

    1. The bill a vendor sends when a subscription renews
    2. The fee charged for early cancellation
    3. The cost of buying a tool at a discount
    4. The value of the best alternative you give up when you make a choice
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Opportunity cost is not a bill you receive. You notice it only by asking what else the same money or time could have done. Money spent on a subscription cannot be spent elsewhere, and time spent building a tool cannot go to other work. For a small tool the larger opportunity cost is almost always time, which is why the chapter prices your own hours in the same currency as everything else.

    See: Opportunity Cost

    3. How does the chapter define total cost of ownership (TCO)?¶

    1. The sum of every cost of acquiring, using, learning, and maintaining a product over a stated period
    2. The purchase price plus the first year's fee
    3. The vendor's cost of developing the product
    4. The price of the product divided by the number of years of use
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. TCO collects purchase or build cost, recurring fees, maintenance, and learning into one comparable figure over a stated horizon. The time terms use the value of an hour, which is how opportunity cost enters the formula. Option B counts only part of the costs. Option C is the vendor's view, not the owner's, and option D is a per-year average of the price that ignores fees, upkeep, and learning.

    4. Why might a program that worked perfectly last year fail after an operating system update, according to the chapter?¶

    1. Software wears out through use like a machine
    2. Vendors deliberately disable old programs
    3. Software does not wear out, but its surroundings change, and a program that does not change with them slowly stops working
    4. Updates always delete user programs
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Software maintenance is the ongoing work of keeping software useful after delivery, including corrective, adaptive, security, and enhancement work. Adaptive maintenance responds to changes in the operating system or hardware. Option A is wrong because software does not physically wear out. Options B and D attribute intent or effects the chapter does not claim. Even a personal tool needs upkeep, as the book's Command Line Tools fix showed.

    Concept Tested: Software Maintenance

    See: Software Maintenance and Its Cost

    5. Why does learning cost help explain why people stay with a product they no longer like?¶

    1. Learning a product always takes longer than a year
    2. The effort already invested seems lost on leaving, and learning a new tool can look more expensive than a year of fees
    3. Vendors forbid users from learning competing tools
    4. Learning cost is waived for users who stay
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Learning cost is the time and effort needed to use a tool well, valued in money or hours. In the chapter's example, 4 hours at $40 is $160, more than a year of the $90 fee. It is paid up front and again after interface redesigns. Option A overgeneralizes, and options C and D invent policies. A tool you generated for your own job lowers this cost because you already know what it does.

    6. In the chapter's break-even analysis, which input matters most in deciding whether the generated tool is ever cheaper than the subscription?¶

    1. The maintenance time of the generated tool
    2. The color of the application icon
    3. The number of features in the subscription product
    4. The vendor's marketing budget
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The generated tool costs more up front in this example ($54 including setup) and then accumulates maintenance time at the value of an hour. If upkeep stays near one hour a year, it overtakes the subscription in about ten weeks. If upkeep rises to three hours a year, its yearly cost of $120 exceeds the subscription's $110, and it never catches up. The other options do not enter the TCO formula.

    7. An agent session takes 70 minutes, and an hour of your time is worth $40. What is the opportunity cost of that time?¶

    1. $14.00
    2. $28.00
    3. $70.00
    4. About $46.67
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Divide 70 minutes by 60 to get 1.167 hours and multiply by $40, which gives about $46.67. That is more than the $14 of API fees for the session, so the honest cost of building the tool includes both. Option A is the fee figure alone, and option C wrongly treats minutes as dollars. Pricing your own hours in the same currency as fees keeps comparisons honest.

    Concept Tested: Opportunity Cost

    See: Opportunity Cost

    8. You expect to spend one hour a year keeping a small tool working, and an hour is worth $40. What is the maintenance cost over five years?¶

    1. $40
    2. $14
    3. $200
    4. $450
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Maintenance cost is hours per year times the value of an hour times the number of years: 1 × $40 × 5 = $200. That is more than twelve times the $14 it cost to generate, which is why a build price alone would mislead. Option A is a single year, and option D is the five-year subscription fee for three computers, not a maintenance figure.

    See: Software Maintenance and Its Cost

    9. Using C = n × f × y, what is the subscription cost for 3 computers at $30 per computer per year over 10 years?¶

    1. $900
    2. $90
    3. $300
    4. $450
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Multiply 3 computers by $30 and by 10 years: 3 × $30 × 10 = $900, a hundred times the original $9 license. Option B is one year, option D is five years, and option C ignores the number of computers. The chapter stresses stating your horizon honestly, because a tool you keep for a decade should be judged on ten years, not one.

    10. If the generated tool needed three hours of upkeep a year instead of one, why would it never catch up with the subscription?¶

    1. Its build price would double
    2. The subscription would become free
    3. Learning cost would disappear for the subscription
    4. Its yearly cost of $120 would exceed the subscription's yearly growth of $110, so the gap would widen each year
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. In the chapter's model, the subscription starts at $40 of learning time and grows by $110 a year from fees and half an hour of upkeep. The generated tool starts at $54 and, at three hours of upkeep at $40, grows by $120 a year. Because it starts higher and grows faster, the curves never cross. This is why maintenance time is the input to test hardest.
